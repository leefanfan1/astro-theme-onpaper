I have come to think that listening to music is a very private thing.

The same song is background noise in someone else's car and an entire stretch of your life in your own headphones. The drum fill lands exactly as the train pulls in. On the way home from a late shift, one line of the chorus says the thing you did not manage to say all day, and for a moment you wonder whether the person who wrote it read your diary.

::music{url="https://open.spotify.com/album/2X2WYTQl5BVInM0StQChCy"}

Two records have been on repeat lately. One is old, one is new. They do not have much in common except that both, at some point, made me turn the city down.

## The old one

The old one I found in a second-hand shop, on a shelf of things nobody wanted. It is not a record anyone would call important. The production is thin and the mix is strange and there is a string section on two tracks that does not belong there. I have listened to it perhaps forty times and I still could not tell you what half the songs are about.

What I can tell you is that the opening track starts with eleven seconds of room noise before anything is played, and that those eleven seconds are the reason I keep putting it on. It sounds like a room with people in it. It sounds like a place I have been.

## The new one

The new one is the opposite: precise, close-miked, every second accounted for. It rewards attention in a way the old one does not — you cannot half-listen to it, because the thing you would miss is usually the thing that was the point.

I do not think one way of listening is better. But I notice that I reach for the old record when I am tired and the new one when I am not, and that seems like a reasonable way to let music organise a week.

## Why the city gets quieter

There is a practical answer. Traffic, construction, the announcements on the platform — all of it is loud, and headphones are a way of choosing what you hear.

There is a less practical answer. A city asks you to be a person in public: to notice, to move aside, to perform being fine. A record asks nothing of you. For the length of an album you are somewhere the city cannot follow, and I think that is worth the risk of missing your stop.
